Yes, cellular automata (CA) are indeed capable of modelling phase transitions, and they are widely used across various scientific disciplines for this purpose. Their discrete nature, simple local rules, and emergent complex behaviour make them a powerful tool for simulating such phenomena.

Here's how cellular automata model phase transitions and examples of such applications:

### Fundamental Principles for Modelling Phase Transitions

1. **Discrete Representation of Space and Time**: CA operate on a discrete grid of cells, evolving in discrete time steps. Each cell exists in one of a finite number of possible states. This inherent discreteness allows CA to simplify complex systems into manageable units, making them particularly suited for modelling phase transitions where macroscopic properties change abruptly.
2. **Local Interaction Rules**: The state of a cell at the next time step is determined by the current states of its local neighbourhood according to a uniform rule. These simple, local interactions can give rise to complex global behaviours, including self-organisation, which is a hallmark of many phase transitions.
3. **Emergent Behaviour and Universality Classes**: Despite their simplicity, CA can exhibit a vast range of complex behaviours. Stephen Wolfram classified CA into four universality classes, where Class 4 is thought to be computationally universal and capable of complex, long-lasting structures, often found at the "edge of chaos". This "edge of chaos" is reminiscent of phase transitions in thermodynamics. CA in different classes can exhibit behaviour analogous to **limit points (Class 1)**, **limit cycles (Class 2)**, and **chaotic attractors (Class 3)** in continuous dynamical systems.
4. **Stochastic and Probabilistic CA**: While many CA are deterministic, **probabilistic cellular automata (PCA)** allow for stochastic transition functions, where the local rule describes a probability distribution of outcomes. This is particularly useful for modelling physical systems where parameters need to be adjusted continuously or where random fluctuations are essential, such as in statistical physics and phase transitions. The probabilistic aspects can enter through probabilistic initial conditions or by modifying the cell update rules.
5. **Perturbations and Ergodicity**: CA can be used to study how systems respond to noise or small perturbations in initial conditions. Phase transitions are often characterized by a change in the dynamics' structure, for instance, when the system is no longer ergodic, leading to what is called **ergodicity breaking**. Recent research has even presented perturbed CA with at least two phase transitions, demonstrating complex responses to varying noise levels.

### Examples of Phase Transitions Modeled by Cellular Automata

- **Statistical Mechanics and Ising Models**: CA are well-suited for dynamical simulations of **Ising models**, where each cell represents a spin (up or down). By adjusting model parameters, the proportion of cells in the same state can be varied to explain phenomena like **ferromagnets demagnetizing when heated**. The **phase transition in the two-dimensional Ising model** has been of particular interest, and its study can be transferred to other phase transitions, such as the **evaporation of a liquid into a gas**. The parallel version of the Ising model has also been defined in CA, though this specific model may not show any phase transition.
- **Fluid Dynamics and Lattice Gas Automata**: **Lattice Gas Cellular Automata (LGCA)** were among the earliest applications of CA, using discrete particles on a lattice to model fluids. With appropriate rules, LGCA can recover macroscopic equations like the Navier-Stokes equations. These models can simulate **phase separations in two-phase fluids** by introducing interactions between nearest neighbours, similar to classical dynamical Ising models, which produce surface tension effects at interfaces.
- **Materials Science**: CA are extensively used to simulate **microstructural evolution** during processes like **crystal growth, solidification, and recrystallization**. These simulations track the kinetics, microstructure, and texture of materials, capturing the evolution of interfaces and transformations.
- **Biological Systems**: CA model various biological pattern formation, such as mollusc shells, plant stomata, and cephalopod skin patterns. More complex biological phenomena, including **metastatic invasion, tumour proliferation, and phenotypic switching**, can be modelled by CA with more complex state spaces and rules. These can exhibit phase transitions related to collective cell migration and development of aggressive carcinomas.
- **Traffic Flow Models**: CA can effectively model **traffic flow**, including the **transition from moving traffic to jamming traffic**, by capturing micro-level dynamics and relating them to macro-level behaviour.
- **Self-Organized Criticality (SOC)**: CA have been used to model systems exhibiting SOC, where systems spontaneously evolve to a critical state without fine-tuning of parameters. This concept is linked to 1/f noise and power-law distributions of events.
- **Specific Transition Types**:
    - **Density phase transitions** (first and second order), where an order parameter (like density of 'ones') changes value at a critical point.
    - **Damage spreading**, where small differences in initial conditions propagate and expand throughout the system.
    - **Asynchronism-induced transitions** and **synchronization phenomena**, where the timing of cell updates influences the system's global state.
    - **Directed percolation (DP) phase transitions**, which are robust across various microscopic dynamic rules and characterized by a continuous transition from an active phase to a stable absorbing state.
    - **Chaotic phase transitions**, which exhibit complex, non-periodic behaviour and may involve bifurcation diagrams.

In summary, cellular automata provide a flexible and robust framework for exploring and simulating phase transitions by transforming continuous physical space and dynamics into discrete, interacting units governed by local rules. This allows for the observation of emergent collective behaviors and the study of critical phenomena in diverse systems.

Think of it like a vast pixel art canvas where each pixel (cell) follows a few simple rules about how its color changes based on its neighbours. By changing these rules or the "temperature" (a parameter), the entire canvas can suddenly transform from a random scattering of colours to an organized pattern, or even "melt" from solid shapes into a chaotic, swirling fluid, much like how real-world materials undergo phase changes.
